# 2D dimension and layout measurement of printed graphics: classical vs learned (late 2026)

Use case assumed throughout: white opaque lettering ("betway", letters 30-60 mm tall, one line of tiny lot text) on clear film about 100 x 300 mm; 5 MP colour camera 2448 x 2048, 12 mm entocentric lens at about 350 mm, about 0.17 mm/px, images rectified to a fixed mm/px with a checkerboard calibration; tolerance about +/-0.5 to 1 mm; current method is threshold + OpenCV template matching.

Source-quality note for the report writer: items marked "(snippet)" come from a search-result summary of the page and were not read in full. Items without that mark were read in full (web page or PDF text). Numbers under "Inferences" are my own arithmetic from the cited rules and the stated setup, not published figures.

## 1. Classical sub-pixel measurement: what accuracy do sources report, and what limits it?

### Takeaway
Published and vendor figures for classical sub-pixel edge location and shape matching cluster between 1/4 px (conservative, edge tools on real parts) and 1/20 to 1/40 px (pattern/shape matching or well-focused, low-noise edges). The limits are focus blur, noise, overexposure, edge quality and the calibration target, not the algorithm.

### Cited Findings
- Hagara and Kulla (MEASUREMENT 2013) compared four 1-D sub-pixel edge detectors: erf-fit (AEF), gray-level moment (GLM, Tabatabai-Mitchell), spatial moment (SM, Lyvers), wavelet (WT). Simulation at SNR 30 dB, 31 samples around the edge, 2000 trials. Standard deviation of edge location error in px, for blur sigma = 0.75 / 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 px: AEF 0.029 / 0.032 / 0.048 / 0.060 / 0.082; GLM 0.030 / 0.033 / 0.050 / 0.720 / 0.127; SM 0.083 / 0.082 / 0.082 / 0.115 / 0.241; WT 0.093 / 0.087 / 0.097 / 0.122 / 0.234. The GLM value 0.720 at sigma = 3 is printed that way in the paper's Table 1 but is inconsistent with its neighbours and with the experimental table, so it is probably a typo for about 0.07. — [Hagara & Kulla, MEASUREMENT 2013](https://www.measurement.sk/M2013/doc/proceedings/311_Hagara-1.pdf)
- Same paper, real images (straight edge of an engine valve, error = residual from a fitted line), blur sigma = 0.84 / 1.54 / 2.18 / 3.25 / 4.25 px: AEF 0.031 / 0.043 / 0.060 / 0.075 / 0.092 px; GLM 0.031 / 0.043 / 0.060 / 0.094 / 0.129 px; SM 0.045 / 0.045 / 0.062 / 0.129 / 0.236 px; WT 0.054 / 0.047 / 0.063 / 0.144 / 0.265 px. — [Hagara & Kulla, MEASUREMENT 2013](https://www.measurement.sk/M2013/doc/proceedings/311_Hagara-1.pdf)
- Their conclusion: for well-focused images (sigma < 1) erf-fit and gray-level moment are the most accurate; for slight defocus (1 <= sigma < 2.5) all four are roughly equal; for strong defocus (sigma >= 2.5) erf-fit is the most accurate but slowest. So defocus alone triples the edge error (about 0.03 px to about 0.09 px). — [Hagara & Kulla, MEASUREMENT 2013](https://www.measurement.sk/M2013/doc/proceedings/311_Hagara-1.pdf)
- Ulrich and Steger (ISPRS 2002) compared normalized cross-correlation, PatMax, modified Hough transform, HALCON shape-based matching, and shape-based matching plus least-squares adjustment on an IC moved by a stage (1 um shift accuracy; shifts of about 1/7 px; 10 images per position). Position errors were "in most cases smaller than 1/20 pixel" and "very similar" across NCC, PatMax, modified Hough, shape-based matching and least-squares adjustment. Orientation: least-squares adjustment reduced the maximum error to 1/50 deg from about 1/16 deg for plain shape-based matching; the best candidates reached 1/50 and 1/100 deg; three others were around 1/6 deg. Hausdorff-distance matching had no sub-pixel output. — [Ulrich & Steger, ISPRS 2002](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIV/part3/papers/paper039.pdf)
- Cognex In-Sight documentation: "The Edge tools are accurate to .25 (1/4) pixels, while the PatMax tools are accurate to .025 (1/40) pixels." and "Accuracy to .10 (1/10) pixels is possible when the part being inspected is flat and has extremely well defined edges." — [Cognex, Calibration Accuracy](https://docs.cognex.com/is_580/web/EN/ise/Content/How_To/Calibration/CalibrationAccuracy.htm)
- The same Cognex page lists what degrades accuracy: rough or burred edges; calibration grids printed on an ordinary inkjet or laser printer; lens quality (high-quality telecentric lenses give the best result and need the least correction); vibration of the mount causing drift; excessive gain (pixel jitter) and overexposure. "Accuracy is severely degraded when these problems are allowed to aggregate." and "The only way to determine the accuracy of your system is to test it." — [Cognex, Calibration Accuracy](https://docs.cognex.com/is_580/web/EN/ise/Content/How_To/Calibration/CalibrationAccuracy.htm)
- Cognex VisionPro PatMax guidance (snippet): pick pattern features that are not aligned with the pixel grid; any blur at train time or run time reduces accuracy significantly. — [Cognex VisionPro, PatMax Accuracy Maximization](https://docs.cognex.com/vpro_980/web/EN/help/html/d65f63c3-490e-4a49-9da3-125054771348.htm)
- MVTec marketing (snippet): HALCON matching finds objects "with an accuracy better than 1/20 pixel" even when partly occluded; an MVTec article mentions applications that need 1/20 px localisation accuracy and 1/100 px repeatability. Vendor claims, test conditions not stated. — [HALCON brochure via Stemmer](https://ftp.stemmer-imaging.com:443/webdavs/docmanager/168136-HALCON-brochure.pdf); [MVTec, A perfect match](https://www.mvtec.com/knowledge-base/machine-vision-insights/a-perfect-match)
- Steger's 1998 evaluation of sub-pixel edge/line extraction (snippet): on real industrial images, accuracy better than 1/10 px is achievable. — [Steger, EEMCV 1998](https://iuks.in.tum.de/_media/members/steger/publications/1998/eemcv-98-steger.pdf)
- Keyence: typical machine-vision repeatability "is about 0.1 pixel under ideal conditions"; they budget 0.5 px as the practical repeatability. Their edge tool projects the image, differentiates the profile, and interpolates the peak from 3 points (peak pixel and two neighbours), reporting in 0.001 px units. — [Keyence, Vision basics: dimension inspection](https://keyence.com/ss/products/vision/visionbasics/basic/practice02)
- NI forum guidance (snippet): with ideal backlighting about 1/10 px is the general expectation; NI claims 1/25 px for its own algorithms. — [NI forum, sub-pixel resolution](https://ni.lithium.com/t5/Machine-Vision/sub-pixel-resolution-vs-intensity-resolution/m-p/2126292)
- Survey-type statements (snippet): sub-pixel methods fall into moment-based, least-squares fitting and interpolation families; moment methods are very precise without noise but degrade with noise; fitting methods depend on the pixel-level detection that seeds them. — [Fabijanska, sub-pixel edge detection overview](https://annals-csis.org/Volume_2/pliks/136.pdf)
- Open-source shape matching: the `meiqua/shape_based_matching` (line2Dup, a LINEMOD-style gradient-orientation matcher) repository offers an "icp + subpixel" branch and claims < 0.1 deg angle accuracy. This is the repo's own claim (snippet); no independent benchmark against HALCON `find_shape_model` was found. — [shape_based_matching mirror](https://gitee.com/hzm8341/shape_based_matching); [SBM_line2D](https://github.com/SeanYan604/SBM_line2D)

### Inferences
- At 0.17 mm/px the published ranges translate to: 1/4 px = 0.043 mm, 1/10 px = 0.017 mm, 1/20 px = 0.0085 mm, 1/40 px = 0.004 mm. Even the conservative 1/4 px figure is about 12x smaller than a +/-0.5 mm tolerance half-band. The edge algorithm is therefore not the bottleneck; the geometry (section 3) is.
- Plain `cv2.matchTemplate` returns the peak on the integer pixel grid (my knowledge of the OpenCV API; not re-checked in this session). Quantisation of +/-0.5 px gives sigma = 1/sqrt(12) = 0.29 px per location, 0.41 px (0.07 mm) for a distance between two matched blocks. A parabola fit around the correlation peak, `cv2.findTransformECC`, or `cv2.phaseCorrelate` are the cheap OpenCV routes to sub-pixel location (API knowledge, not sourced here). The Ulrich-Steger result that NCC with sub-pixel refinement reached about 1/20 px, the same as shape-based matching and PatMax, says the existing template-matching approach can stay if a sub-pixel peak refinement is added.
- Template matching only gives the position of a block. Letter height/width, gaps and stroke width need edge positions: caliper-style 1-D edge profiles across each stroke, or sub-pixel contours. Template matching should be used to place the calipers (localise), not to produce the dimension.
- Stroke width is an edge pair of opposite polarity (dark-to-bright, bright-to-dark). Any bias that moves both edges outward (overexposure/blooming of the white ink, defocus with an asymmetric threshold, a fixed binarisation threshold) adds directly to the width. Centre-to-centre and same-polarity edge distances (left edge of "b" to left edge of "y") largely cancel that bias. So gap and stroke-width checks need a fixed exposure and a 50%-level or gradient-peak edge definition, while pitch/position checks are more forgiving.
- For white ink on clear film the edge contrast depends on what is under the film. A uniform dark matte background gives one clean bright-on-dark edge; a textured or glossy background adds noise that the Hagara numbers (SNR 30 dB) do not cover.

### Gaps
- No single independent benchmark compares Devernay, Steger, Zernike moment, erf fit and caliper tools on the same 2-D data with the same blur/noise. The Hagara table is 1-D only.
- I did not find the HALCON Solution Guide (1D Measuring, 2D Measuring/metrology model) text with stated accuracy conditions; MVTec only publishes those as PDFs and the search returned brochures.
- No accuracy figure was found for OpenCV's generalized Hough or contrib `linemod`, or for feature matching + homography on rigid 2-D parts under controlled lighting.
- No published sub-pixel accuracy for edges of white ink on transparent film specifically (ink edge raggedness, film glare).

## 2. Measurement system analysis: pixels vs tolerance, gauge R&R

### Takeaway
Vendor rules of thumb say the tolerance should span about 10 pixels (or +/-5 px), and gauge R&R should be under 10% of tolerance (10-30% marginal). At 0.17 mm/px a +/-0.5 mm tolerance is only about +/-3 px, so pixel-level measurement fails the rule; sub-pixel edges at 0.1-0.25 px repeatability bring it back inside, and +/-1 mm passes the rule even at pixel level.

### Cited Findings
- Keyence: stable tolerance judgement needs about 10x the repeatability; with 0.5 px practical repeatability the minimum tolerance unit is +/-5 px; "actual tolerance = pixel resolution x 5 pixels". Their example for a 100 mm field: 0.31 MP gives 0.208 mm/px and 1.04 mm; 2 MP gives 0.083 mm/px and 0.415 mm; 21 MP gives 0.024 mm/px and 0.12 mm. — [Keyence, Vision basics: dimension inspection](https://keyence.com/ss/products/vision/visionbasics/basic/practice02)
- Control Engineering (Jon Breen) gives the rule of thumb for linear measurement as "measurement tolerance = 10 pixels" (snippet). — [Control Engineering, Robust vision inspection in 5 steps](https://www.controleng.com/robust-vision-inspection-in-5-steps/)
- A3/Automate (Nello Zuech) (snippet; page returned HTTP 403 on fetch): the measuring instrument should be at least ten times better than the process specification; in machine vision this maps to pixel or sub-pixel increments; some relax repeatability from 10:1 to 5:1; another rule used is that accuracy plus repeatability should be less than one third of the tolerance band. — [Automate, Machine Vision Considerations for Metrology Applications](https://www.automate.org/vision/industry-insights/machine-vision-considerations-for-metrology-applications)
- Quality Magazine (David Dechow) (snippet): engineers often seriously underestimate the number of pixels needed for a desired measurement uncertainty. — [Quality Magazine, Machine Vision Meets Metrology](https://www.qualitymag.com/articles/90926-machine-vision-meets-metrology)
- Gauge R&R acceptance bands attributed to the AIAG MSA manual (4th ed., 2010) by secondary sources (snippet): %GRR < 10% acceptable, 10-30% marginal / may be acceptable depending on application and cost, > 30% unacceptable. %Tolerance (P/T) = 6 x sigma_GRR / (USL - LSL) and is the metric to use when the gauge accepts or rejects parts against a tolerance. Number of distinct categories ndc = 1.41 x sigma_part / sigma_GRR should be 5 or more. — [MetricGate, Gage R&R ANOVA](https://metricgate.com/docs/gauge-rr-anova-msa/); [Quality Magazine, Gage R&R](https://www.qualitymag.com/articles/94385-gage-rr-repeatability-and-reproducibility); [Six Sigma Study Guide](https://sixsigmastudyguide.com/repeatability-and-reproducibility-rr/)
- Cognex: the only way to know a system's accuracy is to test it. — [Cognex, Calibration Accuracy](https://docs.cognex.com/is_580/web/EN/ise/Content/How_To/Calibration/CalibrationAccuracy.htm)

### Inferences
- Pixel count across the tolerance at 0.17 mm/px: +/-0.5 mm = 1.0 mm band = 5.9 px (+/-2.9 px); +/-1.0 mm = 2.0 mm band = 11.8 px (+/-5.9 px).
- Keyence rule (+/-5 px): minimum tolerance at 0.17 mm/px = +/-0.85 mm. So +/-1 mm is just adequate, +/-0.5 mm is not, if one relies on Keyence's 0.5 px practical repeatability. Breen's "tolerance = 10 px" gives the same verdict.
- Gauge R&R target expressed in pixels (P/T = 6 sigma / band): for +/-0.5 mm, P/T < 10% needs sigma < 0.017 mm = 0.10 px and P/T < 30% needs sigma < 0.05 mm = 0.29 px. For +/-1 mm, 10% needs sigma < 0.033 mm = 0.20 px and 30% needs sigma < 0.10 mm = 0.59 px.
- Integer-pixel template matching, from quantisation alone (sigma 0.41 px = 0.07 mm for a distance between two matches), gives 6 sigma = 0.42 mm: P/T = 42% for +/-0.5 mm (fails) and 21% for +/-1 mm (marginal). A caliper/edge measurement at 0.1-0.25 px per edge (0.14-0.35 px for a two-edge distance) gives P/T of about 14-36% for +/-0.5 mm and about 7-18% for +/-1 mm. So sub-pixel edges are what make +/-0.5 mm defensible at this resolution, and only if the practical repeatability is close to the "ideal" 0.1 px end. This counts image-processing repeatability only; perspective and calibration errors (section 3) are systematic and come on top.
- If +/-0.5 mm must be held on every dimension with margin, the options that follow from these rules are: sub-pixel edges plus controlled flatness, or a higher pixel density (two cameras or a 12-20 MP camera to reach about 0.08-0.1 mm/px, which would make +/-0.5 mm span 10-12 px).
- A demo-cell gauge study does not need the full AIAG 10 parts x 3 operators x 3 trials. A type-1 style study is enough to start: measure one golden label 25-50 times with re-placement between shots, report 6 sigma against the tolerance band per dimension, and repeat at the centre and at the edge of the field to expose perspective effects.

### Gaps
- The AIAG MSA manual itself was not read; the 10% / 30% / ndc >= 5 thresholds are from secondary sources that cite it.
- The Automate article (Zuech) could not be fetched (HTTP 403), so its wording comes from the search summary only.
- No source gives a rule specific to printed-graphics or label metrology; all rules found are general machine-vision gauging rules.

## 3. Error budget for a 12 mm entocentric lens at 350 mm; when is telecentric needed?

### Takeaway
With an entocentric lens the scale error from the object leaving the calibrated plane is about h/d, so 1-3 mm of film lift at 350 mm is 0.3-0.9% of the distance from the optical axis. That is 0.4-1.3 mm at the ends of a 300 mm label and is the dominant error term. A telecentric lens for a 300 mm field needs a front element larger than 300 mm, which exists but is large and costly, so flattening the film is the practical fix.

### Cited Findings
- For a non-telecentric lens, a height change h at working distance d with focal length f changes magnification by about h / (d - f), which simplifies to h/d when d is much larger than f. Examples from the page: a 5 mm tall part at 200 mm gives about 2.5%; a 50 mm lens at 500 mm with a 5 mm shift gives about 1.1%. The error is projection geometry, not a lens aberration, so a low-distortion lens still has it. — [Commonlands, What is a telecentric lens](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/what-is-a-telecentric-lens)
- The height-variation error is zero at the image centre and maximal in the image corners. — [Vision-Doctor, measurement error from height variation](https://www.vision-doctor.com/en/service-en/sen-t1/vision-system-calculations/measurement-error-height-variation.html)
- Telecentric lenses are advised for precision gauging where parts cannot sit at a fixed distance, where thickness differences or warp would push dimensions into false accepts/rejects, and where perspective error is a significant fraction of the tolerance budget. They are not advised when parts are fixtured well enough that depth variation is small, or when size, weight or cost rule out a large front element. — [Commonlands, What is a telecentric lens](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/what-is-a-telecentric-lens)
- The front element of a telecentric lens must be at least as large as the object field (a 50 mm field needs at least a 50 mm front element); wide fields make the lens large and expensive. No lens is perfectly telecentric over the whole field; the residual is specified as a maximum chief-ray angle. — [Commonlands, What is a telecentric lens](https://commonlands.com/blogs/technical/what-is-a-telecentric-lens)
- Edmund Optics: "the larger the required field of view, the larger the front optic diameter needs to be"; most machine-vision objects are 50-200 mm; large-FOV telecentrics "can be costly" and trade optical performance against mass, footprint and price. — [Edmund Optics, Large FOV telecentrics](https://www.edmundoptics.eu/knowledge-center/trending-in-optics/large-fov-telecentrics/)
- Edmund's affordable CompactTL telecentrics cover very small fields: the 1X 300 mm WD model covers 8.8 x 6.6 mm and the 2X covers 4.4 x 3.3 mm on a 2/3" sensor; the 2X is listed at 890 USD (snippet). — [Edmund Optics, 2X 300 mm WD CompactTL](https://www.edmundoptics.com/p/2x-300mm-wd-compacttl-telecentric-lens/2839/)
- Opto Engineering TC-series part numbers encode the horizontal field in mm (TC12064 = 64 x 48 mm on a 1/2" sensor; TC12192 = 145 x 109 mm on a 1/3" sensor per its page). Parts with a 308 mm field code exist (TC10M308, TC12M308, TC16M308), and Opto Engineering has announced lenses with a 417 mm front element (snippet). — [Opto Engineering TC series](https://www.opto-e.com/en/products/tc-series); [TC12M308](https://www.opto-e.com/en/products/tc12m-series/TC12M308-F); [Opto Engineering news](https://opto-e.com/en/news/Order-now-the-biggest-telecentric-lenses-and-lights-ever-made-by-Opto-Engineering)
- Cognex: calibration grids printed on standard inkjet or laser printers limit accuracy; telecentric lenses of high quality need the least correction. — [Cognex, Calibration Accuracy](https://docs.cognex.com/is_580/web/EN/ise/Content/How_To/Calibration/CalibrationAccuracy.htm)

### Inferences
- Field of view at 0.17 mm/px: about 416 x 348 mm. A centred 300 mm label has its ends about 150 mm from the optical axis.
- Lift error (d about 350 mm, error = r x h / d, r = distance from the optical axis): at r = 150 mm, h = 1 mm gives 0.43 mm, h = 2 mm gives 0.86 mm, h = 3 mm gives 1.29 mm. A local 1 mm bump under one end of the word therefore uses most of a +/-0.5 mm tolerance by itself.
- Uniform lift (whole label h above the calibrated plane) is a pure scale error h/d: 0.29% per mm. Over a 250 mm word width that is 0.71 mm per mm of lift; over a 50 mm letter height 0.14 mm per mm; over a 5 mm stroke 0.014 mm per mm. Small features (stroke width, single-letter size, letter gaps) are nearly immune to lift; long dimensions (overall word width, print-to-film-edge distances at the ends) are not.
- The same formula applies to the calibration plane offset. If the checkerboard surface sat t mm above the surface the film lies on (board thickness), every dimension is scaled by t/d: a 2 mm thick board gives 0.57%, which is 1.7 mm over 300 mm. This is a constant bias and can be removed by measuring a reference of known length in the working plane (or calibrating with the board surface at film height) and correcting the scale. Film thickness itself (about 0.1 mm or less) contributes about 0.03% and is negligible.
- A golden-sample comparison cancels the constant scale bias as long as the golden sample and the test label lie in the same plane. It does not cancel random curl.
- Budget sketch for the 300 mm dimension at +/-0.5 mm: sub-pixel edges about 0.02-0.05 mm; residual lens distortion after checkerboard calibration typically a few tenths of a pixel (no figure sourced here), about 0.03-0.07 mm; lift of 1 mm about 0.4 mm at the ends. Flatness control to well under 1 mm (target about 0.3 mm, giving about 0.13 mm at r = 150 mm) is what the tolerance requires. Ways to get it: vacuum platen, a clean anti-reflective glass cover plate, or tensioning the film.
- A longer focal length at a longer working distance reduces the error in proportion to d: the same field from 700 mm with a 25 mm lens halves the lift error. This is the cheap intermediate step before telecentric optics.
- Telecentric is justified only if flatness cannot be controlled. For a 300 mm field it means a lens with a front element over 300 mm, which is outside "small demo cell" budget and size.

### Gaps
- No price was found for 300 mm-class telecentric lenses (Opto Engineering lists them on request); "expensive" is qualitative in all sources.
- The Vision-Doctor page is a calculator and does not print its formula; the h/(d - f) form is from Commonlands only (a lens vendor blog, though it is standard pinhole geometry).
- No sourced number for residual distortion after OpenCV checkerboard calibration on a 12 mm lens, for checkerboard print accuracy, or for thermal drift of focal length/mount in a cell like this. These need to be measured (reprojection RMS, a certified scale or glass grid, and a warm-up drift test).

## 4. Learned alternatives: is there evidence they beat classical sub-pixel methods on high-contrast rigid 2-D parts?

### Takeaway
I found no published evidence that learned methods beat classical sub-pixel edges or shape matching on high-contrast, rigid, flat 2-D targets. The papers that report gains do so on low-resolution or low-contrast edges and against weak baselines, and the academic matching benchmarks score accuracy at 1-10 px thresholds, one to two orders of magnitude coarser than classical metrology claims.

### Cited Findings
- Wang et al. (Sensors 2019) trained a 1-D VGG-16 + bidirectional LSTM to regress sub-pixel edge positions for steel-plate length measurement on a cold-rolling line. Images were 2448 x 2048 downsampled 4x; ground truth was manual edge marking at full resolution divided by four, with stated ground-truth error "within one-fourth of a pixel"; 241 training and 62 test images. Reported error: proposed MAE 0.112, RMSE 0.202; quadratic interpolation MAE 0.378, RMSE 0.389; Sobel-Zernike moment MAE 0.355, RMSE 0.386 (units not stated, apparently low-resolution pixels). Only two classical baselines, one production line, one camera. — [Wang et al., Sensors 2019 (PMC6928596)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6928596)
- A 2021 paper in Measurement uses a deep network (HED-style) for rough edge detection and then cubic-spline interpolation for sub-pixel edges on a shaft workpiece, reporting 0.02 mm measuring accuracy (snippet). The learned part localises; the number still comes from classical interpolation. — [An online visual measurement method for workpiece dimension based on deep learning](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2605659846/Details)
- A 2025 arXiv paper proposes a new classical sub-pixel edge localiser (Converted Intensity Summation) and claims higher accuracy and lower run time than the state-of-the-art fitting-based method on synthetic and real images (PCB, remote sensing, medical). It shows that classical sub-pixel localisation is still an active line of work; its abstract gives no numbers. — [arXiv 2502.16502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16502)
- LoFTR evaluates homography estimation on HPatches by the corner error of the warped image at 3, 5 and 10 px thresholds (AUC at 3 px: 65.9 vs 50.6 for R2D2+NN), with OpenCV RANSAC for the homography (snippet). — [LoFTR, arXiv 2104.00680](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.00680)
- LightGlue reports HPatches AUC at 1 px and 5 px with a 3 px inlier threshold; as a sparse matcher its localisation is bounded by the keypoint detector (snippet). Efficient LoFTR adds a two-stage correlation layer for sub-pixel correspondences (snippet). RoMa / RoMa v2 give dense sub-pixel correspondences with a per-pixel error covariance but have a significant runtime and memory footprint (snippet). — [LightGlue, arXiv 2306.13643](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.13643); [Efficient LoFTR, CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Wang_Efficient_LoFTR_Semi-Dense_Local_Feature_Matching_with_Sparse-Like_Speed_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf); [RoMa v2, arXiv 2511.15706](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15706v1)
- "Measure Anything" (2024) uses SAM masks for dimensional measurement of rods (skeleton and line-segment extraction); the authors note that occlusion or overlap can make segmentation fail and give wrong dimensions (snippet). — [Measure Anything, arXiv 2412.03472](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.03472)
- SAM-family models used for metrology-grade contours need task-specific adaptation: SegSEM fine-tunes SAM 2 for SEM contour extraction with an edge-placement-error penalty; LithoSeg uses SAM for a coarse mask and then refines contour points with a separate small network (snippets). — [SegSEM, arXiv 2602.20471](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.20471); [LithoSeg, arXiv 2511.12005](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.12005.md)
- A 2026 ISPRS archive paper on e-waste segmentation found a trained YOLOv8 had stronger boundary precision than SAM 2 (snippet), so zero-shot SAM is not automatically the best boundary source. — [ISPRS Archives XLIX-B3-2026](https://isprs-archives.copernicus.org/articles/XLIX-B3-2026/205/2026/isprs-archives-XLIX-B3-2026-205-2026.pdf)
- Keyence publishes an article on AI in metrology for its image dimension measurement line (title only seen in search; not read). — [Keyence, How AI is changing metrology](https://www.keyence.com/products/measure-sys/image-measure/resources/image-measure-resources/how-ai-is-changing-metrology-and-measurement-accuracy.jsp)

### Inferences
- In the one head-to-head paper found (Wang et al.), the learned method's advantage (0.11 vs 0.36-0.38) is smaller than or comparable to the stated ground-truth uncertainty (up to 0.25 px), the classical baselines score far worse than the 0.03-0.1 px that classical methods reach on clean edges (section 1), and the scene is a low-contrast steel edge on a rolling line. That supports "learned helps when the edge is hard to find", not "learned is more accurate on clean edges".
- Segmentation masks (U-Net, SAM 2) are produced on the pixel grid, often at reduced internal resolution and then upsampled, so the mask boundary is at best pixel-accurate unless a sub-pixel refinement follows. The SAM-for-metrology papers above all add a refinement or boundary-specific training, which matches this.
- For white ink on a uniform dark background, a threshold already segments the letters. A network adds labelling cost, a training set per SKU, and a result that is harder to validate in a gauge study, with no accuracy gain shown in the literature found.
- Where learned components make sense in this project: (a) localising or reading the tiny lot-code line (OCR) and (b) coping with backgrounds or glare where thresholding fails. The millimetre number should still come from classical edges.
- Dense matchers (LoFTR, RoMa, MASt3R) are built for wide-baseline, textured, natural scenes. A flat-colour logo has few texture features away from its edges, and the benchmark thresholds (1-10 px) say nothing about 0.1 px behaviour. They are not a substitute for ECC or shape-based alignment here.

### Gaps
- No paper found that benchmarks keypoint/landmark regression networks, deep homography estimation, or MASt3R against sub-pixel edge tools for 2-D gauging on rigid high-contrast parts. Absence of evidence in a short search, not proof that none exists.
- No published boundary error in pixels for SAM 2 masks on industrial printed graphics.
- No vendor (Cognex, MVTec, Keyence) statement was found saying deep-learning tools should be used for the dimensional value itself; I also did not find an explicit vendor statement saying they should not.

## 5. Comparison against artwork / CAD

### Takeaway
Commercial 2-D vision measuring systems import DXF (and PDF/image drawings), overlay the design on the part and report profile deviation against the CAD or a master sample. The same idea can be built in OpenCV: rasterise or sample the artwork contours at the image scale, register, and compute per-point normal distance from sub-pixel image contours.

### Cited Findings
- Keyence IM-X: measurement programs can be generated from DXF with design values and tolerances loaded in one step; drawings can also be imported as PDF or JPEG; Keyence claims up to 80% less programming labour (snippet, vendor claim). — [Keyence IM-X1000 features](https://www.keyence.com/products/measure-sys/image-measure/im-x1000/details/feature-01.jsp)
- Keyence describes profile-tolerance measurement by comparing the part with CAD drawings or a master sample, and a case study describes overlaying CAD data to get saved deviation values (snippet). — [Keyence image dimension measurement systems](https://www.keyence.com/products/measure-sys/image-measure/); [Keyence IM-X case studies](https://www.keyence.com/resources/case-studies-metal-electronic-parts_689315.jsp)
- For alignment before comparison, Ulrich and Steger show that shape-based matching followed by least-squares adjustment (minimising the distance between model tangents and image edge points) reaches about 1/20 px in position and 1/50 deg in angle. This is the registration step a contour-to-CAD comparison needs. — [Ulrich & Steger, ISPRS 2002](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIV/part3/papers/paper039.pdf)
- MVTec has a success story on using shape-based matching to detect distortions in print inspection (title seen in search; not read). — [MVTec, print inspection success story](https://www.mvtec.com/success-stories-list/detail/correct-detection-of-distortions-during-print-inspection-with-halcons-shape-based-matching)

### Inferences
- Angle error matters over a long label: 1/50 deg over a 150 mm lever arm is 0.05 mm; 1/6 deg (the weaker matchers in Ulrich-Steger) is 0.44 mm. Registration for a 300 mm label should use the whole word (or two far-apart register marks), not one small template.
- OpenCV recipe that mirrors the commercial approach: (1) render the artwork (PDF/DXF/SVG) to a binary image or polylines at the rectified scale (0.17 mm/px, or supersampled); (2) coarse align with the existing template match; (3) refine with ECC (Euclidean motion model) or an ICP-style least-squares fit of image edge points to artwork contours; (4) extract sub-pixel contour points of the print; (5) for each point compute signed distance to the artwork contour, by a distance transform of the rendered artwork for speed or by point-to-polyline normal distance for precision; (6) report max and RMS deviation per letter, which is a profile-tolerance style result, alongside the discrete dimensions (width, height, gaps).
- Fit choice changes the verdict. A best-fit (rigid) alignment removes placement error and shows only shape/size error; allowing scale in the fit would hide a real size error, so scale must stay fixed for a dimension check. Position of the print relative to the film edge or register marks must be measured in a separate datum frame, not after best-fit.
- A golden-sample image can stand in for the artwork and has the advantage of cancelling optical bias (same lens, same plane), but it bakes the golden sample's own deviation into every result. Artwork is the cleaner nominal if the print scale (heat-transfer shrink or stretch) is known.
- Commercial contour-to-CAD tools (HALCON metrology model and contour comparison, Cognex, Keyence IM) save development time; nothing in the method requires them.

### Gaps
- No documentation found on how Keyence IM computes its best fit or contour deviation (algorithm not published in the pages found).
- Nothing found specific to print-to-cut or die-cut registration verification systems and their stated accuracies.
- The HALCON operators for contour-to-model comparison were not confirmed from MVTec documentation in this session.

## 6. Hybrid practice: learned localisation, classical measurement

### Takeaway
The pattern supported by what was found is "locate coarsely (template, shape model or a network), then measure with classical sub-pixel edges". The literature examples that reach about 0.02 mm use a network only for rough edge finding and classical interpolation or moments for the number.

### Cited Findings
- Deep rough edge detection followed by cubic-spline sub-pixel interpolation reports 0.02 mm on a shaft workpiece (snippet). — [Measurement 2021, online visual measurement method](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_journals_2605659846/Details)
- A preprint describes an improved Zernike-moment sub-pixel method with errors under 0.02 mm and a deep-learning-assisted variant for complex backgrounds (snippet; not peer reviewed). — [Preprints.org manuscript](https://www.preprints.org/frontend/manuscript/e49d48726da04cce7f6175ea517b5186/download_pub)
- LithoSeg: SAM for the coarse mask, a separate lightweight stage to refine contour points to metrology precision (snippet). — [LithoSeg, arXiv 2511.12005](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.12005.md)
- Classical coarse-to-fine is the established industrial form of the same idea: shape-based matching gives the pose, least-squares adjustment refines it (maximum angle error from about 1/16 deg down to 1/50 deg). — [Ulrich & Steger, ISPRS 2002](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIV/part3/papers/paper039.pdf)
- Cognex positions the tools the same way: PatMax locates (about 1/40 px), caliper/edge tools measure feature widths by multiple fast edge detections (snippet for the caliper description). — [Cognex, Calibration Accuracy](https://docs.cognex.com/is_580/web/EN/ise/Content/How_To/Calibration/CalibrationAccuracy.htm)

### Inferences
- For this label the localiser does not need to be learned. The current threshold + template match already finds the blocks. The upgrade with the best return is to place 1-D calipers (several parallel scan lines averaged, perpendicular to each stroke edge) at positions defined in the template frame, fit the edge with a gradient-peak parabola or erf, and compute dimensions from those edges. Averaging N scan lines reduces noise by about sqrt(N).
- A learned stage becomes worth it only if a SKU has a background or artwork where thresholding and template matching fail (busy backing, glare, variable ink opacity), or for the lot-code text. That fits the project's existing rule of per-SKU parameters and a per-SKU code path when the shared one fails.
- An anomaly or segmentation model used for surface defects (scratch, stain) can share the image but should not feed the dimension result, because its mask edges are not metrology-grade (section 4).

### Gaps
- No integrator white paper was found that explicitly recommends "deep learning to locate, rule-based tools to measure" in those words; the statement is assembled from the examples above and from how vendors document their tools.
- No accuracy figure for a hybrid pipeline on printed film or labels.

## 7. The nearly invisible clear film edge

### Takeaway
Sources recommend directed (collimated) backlight for precise edges of transparent parts and low-angle dark-field light to make edges and defects scatter, but true collimated backlight pairs best with telecentric lenses and no source gives an accuracy for film edges. Measuring the print relative to printed register marks is the more robust design; film-edge distance should be treated as a coarse check unless the lighting is changed.

### Cited Findings
- Collimated backlight gives a more precise image of object edges than diffuse backlight, which makes the exact edge location harder to determine; collimated light also helps with transparent and semi-transparent objects because the directed rays are refracted more strongly at edges and irregularities (snippet). — [iiM / LUMIMAX, collimated and telecentric backlight](https://iim-ag.com/es/lumimax/knowledge/topic-1-lighting-angle-influence-of-reflected-light-and-backlight-on-the-camera-view/backlight/collimated-and-telecentric-backlight.html)
- A true collimated backlight needs a lens aperture at least as large as the field of view, which in practice limits it to telecentric lenses; "flat collimated" backlights that narrow the emission angle with film layers are not limited to telecentric lenses (snippet). — [Quality Magazine, Machine Vision 101](https://digitaledition.qualitymag.com/december-2022/machine-vision-101)
- Dark-field backlight is suited to picking up defects in transparent objects; low-angle dark-field front light leaves the flat surface dark and shows only features that scatter light to the camera (snippet). — [Quality Magazine, machine vision lighting](https://www.qualitymag.com/articles/97255-machine-vision-lighting-and-why-its-important); [NI forum](https://forums.ni.com/t5/Machine-Vision/What-Type-of-Structured-Light-to-Use-to-Detect-Surface-Features/td-p/3848025/jump-to/first-unread-message)
- For print viewed through transparent film or tape, Keyence recommends diffuse illumination to avoid specular reflection from the film (snippet). — [Keyence, basics of lighting selection](https://www.keyence.com/products/vision/vision-sys/resources/vision-sys-resources/basics-of-lighting-selection.jsp)
- The light should be larger than the part, otherwise the part edges come out dark (snippet, forum answer). — [NI forum](https://forums.ni.com/t5/Machine-Vision/What-Type-of-Structured-Light-to-Use-to-Detect-Surface-Features/td-p/3848025/jump-to/first-unread-message)

### Inferences
- The two lighting needs conflict. The white print wants diffuse front light on a dark background (bright ink, no film glare). The clear film edge wants directional light (low-angle dark field so the cut edge scatters, or a narrow-angle backlight so the edge refracts light away and shows as a thin dark or bright line). A two-shot sequence with two light channels, one image for the print and one for the film outline, is the usual way to serve both with one camera.
- The film edge is also where the measurement geometry is worst: it is the farthest point from the optical axis and the part of the film most likely to curl, so lift error (section 3) peaks there. Even with a visible edge, expect the print-to-edge distance to be the least repeatable dimension.
- Design alternatives that avoid the edge: (a) printed register marks or a printed outline on the carrier, measured with the same lighting as the lettering; (b) seat the film against mechanical stops or pins and measure print position relative to the fixture datum, visible as a fixed feature in the image; (c) if the cut is made by a die registered to the print, verify print-to-cut on a sample basis with a dedicated setup instead of in-line.
- If the edge must be measured in-line, fit a straight line to many edge points along each film side (robust fit) instead of using single points; a line fit through hundreds of noisy points gives a far more stable edge than any single caliper.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies edge-location accuracy for clear PET or heat-transfer carrier film under any lighting.
- No source found on polarisation-based methods (crossed polarisers showing birefringent PET film) for film outline detection; it is a known trick but I did not find a citable application note in this session.
- All lighting statements are from vendor notes or forum answers; none was tested on this label. A lighting trial on real samples is needed before committing.
